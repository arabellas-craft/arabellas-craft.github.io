There are many things you'll hear repeated in popular explainers on Samhain (pronounced [SAH-win](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/Samhain){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}). The veil between worlds is at its thinnest. The Celts believed the dead came back that night. Samhain was the Celtic New Year. People wore disguises so the spirits wouldn't know them. The Church put All Saints' Day on November 1st to cover the festival up.

Most of them have something real underneath, which is why they've traveled so well. The popular version mostly goes wrong on the dates (like when the dead return) and the reasons (why disguises are worn).

This article follows the Irish sources to trace back these customs. For the medieval tales I use Jeffrey Gantz's translation,[^1] and Mark Williams on the gods in that literature.[^2] For the folk record I use Kevin Danaher's *The Year in Ireland* (1972)[^3] and the [Dúchas Schools' Collection](https://duchas.ie){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, gathered in 1937–39. For the history of the claims themselves I use Robert Davis on Halloween as a Christian festival,[^7] and Ronald Hutton, both his *Stations of the Sun* (1996) and his 2024 article in *Folklore*.[^4][^5]

## The Thin Veil: The Barrier Is Old, the Thinning Night Isn't

A barrier between this world and the Otherworld runs all through the medieval tales, but it doesn't get thinner once a year. In those stories the divide is permanent, and the earliest version of the one-night thinning I can find was published in 1940.

*Serglige Con Culaind*, "The Wasting Sickness of Cú Chulaind," is set at Samhain. It ends with the sea god Manannán mac Lir separating two lovers for good: "\[Manannán\] shook his cloak between Cú Chulaind and Fand, that they might never meet again."[^1] The barrier went up at Samhain, rather than thinning.

The tales treat that divide as constant. Williams describes a fourteenth-century tale in which Eithne, swimming in the Boyne, "accidentally slips out of the haze of invisibility that keeps the Túatha Dé Danann hidden from mortals." In Tochmarc Étaíne, "The Wooing of Étaín," he finds Midir saying the people of the síd are there invisibly "on every side," their world "superimposed upon our own like a sheet of tracing paper."[^2]

The early sources do show Samhain as a busy time in the Otherworld. Hutton describes it in the medieval literature as a time "when supernatural and superhuman beings were especially active, and inclined to interfere with humans." He names *Mesca Ulaid*, "The Intoxication of the Ulaid," and *Echtra Nerai*, "The Adventure of Nera," alongside the two tales above as examples.[^5]

The earliest source I can find mentioning the thinning barrier at Samhain is Marie-Louise Sjoestedt's Celtic Gods and Heroes, which puts the barrier, the date and the New Year into one passage. The two worlds are "distinct and mutually almost impervious," but "there is a time when the invisible magic partition which separates them is withdrawn... This happens during the night of Samhain (from the thirty-first of October to the first of November), the eve of the Celtic New Year."[^6]

## The Returning Dead: In the Folk Record, They Come as the Holy Souls
The dead do come back in the Irish folk record. But they come on All Souls' Night, November 2nd, and they come as Christian souls let out of Purgatory for the night.

Search the Dúchas Schools' Collection for All Souls' Night and you'll find dozens of accounts of candles in windows, doors left open, a place kept ready. One account from Donegal describes the custom:
> "After twelve o'clock not a human being would stir out of the house, as it is believed that the poor Souls are allowed out of Purgatory for that night... The old people leave the door on the latch, a bright fire on the hearth, the house is tidy, and clean, a pipe, and tobacco, and a box of snuff, all ready to welcome any poor soul who should choose to visit the house that night."[^8]

A Cork account gives the date:
> "All Souls' Night is really the 'eve' of November 2nd... They believed that the Poor Souls used to leave Purgatory that night and roam the world asking prayers of friends."[^9]

Search the same collection for Halloween and you mostly get games. Apples on a string, the three saucers a blindfolded player picks from, the ring in the brack, cabbage, colcannon, watching which way the wind blows. Most of the Halloween accounts I read don't mention the dead at all, and the Irish-language ones for Oíche Shamhna are mostly fairy stories rather than human ghosts.

That's not universal. A Galway account puts the dead on Halloween:
> "at midnight on hallowee'n the souls in Purgatory were released and before entering Heaven they returned to their earthly haunts. In many parts of the country bread and water are left on the hearth in case some poor souls would be hungry."[^10]

But wherever the dead turn up in these accounts, they come as the Holy Souls. The theology is Christian on both nights. 

Danaher finds the same pattern across the wider folk collections. Most people, he says, "regarded the night of 2 Nov., All Souls Day, and not that of Hallow E'en, as the time of the visit of the dead."[^3] Dáithí Ó hÓgáin calls Samhain the festival of the dead, then admits in the same sentence that All Souls has taken it over. The day keeps its old feel, he writes, "although the Feast of All Souls two days later has in recent centuries tended to submerge it."[^11]

The medieval material is thinner still. Hutton finds no clear link between the festival and the human dead in any of the literatures that set out to portray pagan times. He adds his own limit straight after: "It is likely that the pre-Christian societies of ancient northern Europe, in common with so many across the world, had an annual festival to commemorate those dead, but no direct evidence for one survives."[^5]

The idea that Samhain was a feast of the dead didn't come out of the Irish material. It came from Sir James Frazer, the Scottish anthropologist whose The Golden Bough (1890) taught a generation of readers to look for a pagan original underneath every Christian festival. Frazer turned that method on All Souls' in 1905. His conclusion was that All Souls' is "nothing but an old pagan feast of the dead which the Christian, unable or unwilling to suppress, resolved from reasons of policy to connive at."[^5]

The Irish record doesn't bear that out. The dead in these accounts are the Holy Souls, they come out of Purgatory, and they come on the night the Church gave them. Frazer laid a theory over the evidence rather than reading one out of it.


## The Celtic New Year: A Victorian Suggestion That Became Standard

No Irish source says the year began at Samhain. The claim starts as one scholar's hesitant suggestion in 1847, gets repeated by bigger names until it hardens, and now gets printed as established fact.

John O'Donovan, the Irish scholar who translated the Annals of the Four Masters, made that suggestion while reading an entry about the word for autumn in Sanas Cormaic, a glossary put together around 900. He thought the year might have ended there, and he hedged it: "if there be no error of transcribers." [^5]

Forty years later, Sir John Rhys, the first professor of Celtic at Oxford, took it up in lectures published in 1892. He brought in Halloween divination from Ireland and Wales, plus the story of a bonfire at Tlachtga, a hill in Co. Meath, that all Ireland's fires were relit from. In 1901 he added folklore he'd collected on the Isle of Man. Leases there ended in late October, and an old man remembered a farmer telling him Halloween had once been New Year's Eve. By 1905 Rhys could call it a proven fact.[^5]

Davis has a plainer reading of the Manx material. Those Halloween revels look like January 1st games copied onto a second night for extra fun. That happens often enough in festive culture.[^7]

Frazer picked up the New Year and used it to prop up his feast of the dead. Twelve years later he used the feast of the dead to prop up the New Year.[^5] Each claim was serving as the evidence for the other. After that it circulated on its own. Hutton lists four respected scholars restating it in the 1990s with nothing behind it:

> "None of these authors provided any evidence or source references for these statements: they were repeating something so well known and established that it apparently required no substantiation."[^5]

His verdict is that the evidence "is all indirect, speculative, and inconclusive." The claim, he says, "should no longer be restated as proven fact."[^5]

The Irish new-year customs that do survive in the folk record are on January 1st, and I've written them up: the first footing, the cake against hunger, the wind-watching of [Lá Coille](/irish-new-year-folk-magic-la-coille). The farming year turned on February 1st. The legal year began on March 25th until 1751. Ireland had several year-starts on record, and Samhain isn't among them.

## The Disguises: The Custom Is Recorded, and It Was About Charity

People did go masked from door to door at Halloween. Guising, as it's called, is well documented. But the masks went with begging, and the begging was for the souls in Purgatory, so this was a charity custom before it was anything to do with hiding from spirits.

Davis argues that the giveaway isn't the mask but what goes with it: "the determining factor in disguise may not be masquerade itself but the action of begging which almost invariably accompanies it."[^7] Going door to door at this season was tied to alms for the souls in purgatory. That's the same purgatory the Holy Souls come out of in the Dúchas accounts above. Charity counted as penance, and giving in secret counted for more, which is a reason to cover your face. Davis describes Halloween in the early modern sources as "a communal celebration in which the duties of the rich to the poor are conscientiously discharged."[^7]

The songs the guisers sang are the clearest evidence of it:

> A soul, a soul, a soul cake
> Please, good missus, a soul cake
> An apple, a pear, a plum or a cherry
> Any good thing to make us all merry
>
> ...
>
> The lanes are very dirty and my shoes are very thin
> I've got a little pocket I can put a penny in
> If you haven't got a penny, a ha' penny will do
> If you haven't got a ha' penny, then God bless you.[^7]

The Irish material describes the same thing without any spirits in it. The National Museum of Ireland's folklife collection notes that "ghostly masks were made to frighten neighbours on Hallowe'en and bands of men or children liberated by their disguises, often went visiting and entertaining."[^12] Danaher records a Halloween custom of dressing up as ghosts "to frighten some young relatives." That came out of a local fear of one haunting, not a general dread of the dead.[^3]

None of this rules out the older reading. Davis lists three reasons that have been offered for guising, including hiding from spirits. He allows that "there may be strands of all three motivations in the practice, and local factors may well be more influential than any general theory of ritual."[^7] What the record won't support is the spirit story as the only one.

## All Saints' Day: A Northern European Feast Before It Was an Irish One

The usual story is that the Church moved All Saints' Day to November 1st to paper over Samhain. Which pope did it, and when, changes with the telling.

The November 1st feast started in the Germanic churches of northern Europe, where Samhain was not a festival or tradition. Ireland already had its own All Saints' Day, on April 20th, and Irish clergy held onto it for more than a generation after the November date showed up. The emperor Louis the Pious made November 1st official across most of north-west Europe in 835, at Pope Gregory IV's prompting, and Gregory was signing off on a practice that had already started in the churches of England and Germany.[^4]

Hutton notes that the November feast "had not, however, started in Ireland, where the Felire of Oengus and the Martyrology of Tallaght prove that the early medieval churches celebrated the feast of All Saints upon 20 April." That, he says, "makes nonsense of Frazer's notion that the November date was chosen because of 'Celtic' influence."[^4] Both Rome and the Irish churches followed a Germanic idea.

So the date traveled towards Ireland rather than out of it, and the Irish were among the last to take it up. If the point had been to cover an Irish festival, Ireland is an odd place to meet resistance.

## Where the Pagan Version Came From

The pagan Samhain most people know was assembled by Victorian and Edwardian writers who had their own argument to make about Christianity. It's stayed in circulation because two groups with opposite goals both find it useful: people who want Halloween to be pagan and dangerous, and people who want it to be pagan and theirs. Davis opens his chapter on that point: the two camps disagree about nearly everything except the premise.[^7]

My own shelf isn't exempt. Gantz's introduction to *Early Irish Myths and Sagas*, a translation I cite and recommend, gets three of these five claims into two sentences:

> "The first day of November, called Samuin, was both the first day of winter and the first day of the new year; the feast has since given rise to Hallowe'en/All Saints' Day and contributed the bonfire to Guy Fawkes celebrations. Samuin was a day of changes, of births and deaths; it was an open door between the real world and the otherworld."[^1]

That's a careful scholar in a serious introduction, repeating what everyone knew.

Hutton has done more than anyone to take these claims apart, and he stops short of a verdict. Neither idea, he writes, "has been conclusively disproved, and probably never will be: it is notoriously difficult to prove a negative in many areas of cultural history."[^5]

None of this is a reason to mark the day differently. The folk record gives you something more specific than the usual story: souls out of Purgatory, a fire kept in, a house made ready for whoever might call. The customs are old. What's recent is the explanation stacked on top of them.
