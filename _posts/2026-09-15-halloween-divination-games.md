When Irish schoolchildren wrote about the night they called Hallow Eve for the Dúchas Schools' Collection in 1937, what they recorded from their parents and grandparents were fortune-telling games. Who will I marry? Will I emigrate? Will I be alive this time next year?

These games belong to Oíche Shamhna (pronounced [EE-huh HOW-nuh](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/O%C3%ADche_Shamhna){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}), the eve of [Samhain](/what-is-samhain) on October 31st. In *[The Year in Ireland](https://bookshop.org/a/84772/9781856350938){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}*, Kevin Danaher records that the games gave the night some of its English names: Snap-Apple Night, Ducking Night, Nut-crack Night, and in Irish Oíche na nÚll, the night of the apples.[^1]

## The three saucers

The most common rite in the folk record involves three saucers on a table. Clay in one, water in the second, a ring in the third. A player is blindfolded, led to the table, and reaches for one. The clay means death inside the year, the water means crossing the sea, and the ring means marriage.[^2]

A Carlow account: 
> "If he touched the clay it meant he would die before the year was out. Touching the water signifies crossing water and touching the ring meant a marriage."[^3] 

A Leitrim account titles the whole game "Death, Exile or Marriage."[^4]

A Cavan account has the plates moved around after the player is blindfolded, so nobody can count their way to the ring.[^5] A Kerry account in Irish sets four plates, adding a rosary beads; reach that one and you'll enter the church instead of a marriage.[^6] Some houses added dirty water for a marriage to an older person, or milky water for marrying a widow.[^7]

## The ring in the cake

In another popular game a ring goes into a brack, a cake or the [colcannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colcannon){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, the pieces are handed around, and whoever finds it marries first. A Cavan convent school recorded both in one sitting: colcannon with a ring in it for dinner, and brack with rings in them for the tea.[^8]

At Altar school in west Cork, cakes were baked with rings, rags, peas and beans in them, each with a fixed reading: the ring for a marriage that year, the rag for poverty, the pea for wealth, the bean for happiness.[^9] A Clare account in Irish has the woman of the house making an apple cake the night before with a ring, a thimble and a sixpence in it: the ring means you will get married first, the thimble means you'll marry a tailor, and the sixpence means you will be rich for life.[^10]

## Nuts in the fire

Two nuts are selected to represent each half of a couple in the room and set on the hearth or the hob. If one jumps away from the other, they won't marry.[^2]

In a Kerry account in Irish, if a nut jumps *out of the fire* that means no marriage that year, and a pair that stay together that means a wedding.[^11] In Clare they used beans instead of nuts, laid in pairs by the fire under a couple's names.[^10]

## Apple peels

Peel an apple in a single unbroken strip, throw it over your left shoulder, and the shape the peel lands in will be your future spouse's initial.[^12]

##  Salt at bedtime

Eat something salty before bed, and the person you'll marry will offer you water in your dreams.

An account from Cavan uses an egg:
> Boil an egg hard, take out the yolk, fill the empty shell with salt, and eat the salt, the shell and the white before going to bed. A picture of your future husband is in the dream that follows.[^12]

The Donegal version is a dare. A girl takes a bite of a raw herring in the devil's name, and if she wakes thirsty in the night, her intended is the one who brings her the drink.[^7]

## The cabbage garden

Three girls walk into a cabbage garden together, catch hands at the gate, walk backwards to the bed, and each pulls a cabbage by the roots with her hands behind her back. A gnarled root means an elderly husband, a straight one means a young and handsome one.[^13] Donegal has a version for boys: pull the third head in the third row, hang it over the door, and the first girl through it is your intended.[^7]


## Other divination methods

The Dúchas Schools' Collection is full of folk divination practices that were done around Samhain.

- Pull yarrow on the way home from school or work and sleep with it under your pillow, and you'll dream of the person you'll marry.[^14]
- Shut a snail under a plate of flour overnight, and read the initials it writes crossing the flour by morning.[^15]
- Pour molten lead through the head of a key into a basin of cold water, and read the shape it sets into for the position in life you'll take up.[^4] (If you want to try this one, use wax not lead!)
- Put an ivy leaf under your pillow for the same dream.[^16]
- Wash a shirt in a south-running stream after sunset and leave it on a chair by the fire to dry, and the person you'll marry comes in at twelve o'clock to turn it.[^17]


## Trying them now

Most of these games can be played with every day household items. These games were played in a full room, out loud, with everyone leaning in to watch, and the answers were only ever as serious as the company made them.
