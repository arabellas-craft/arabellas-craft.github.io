Tarot is bad at yes or no questions. Tarot has no built-in mechanism for producing yes or no answers. You can find systems for reading them but the popular rules contradict each other and people often find they don't work well or consistently.

That doesn't make the question bad or wrong to ask. Sometimes a straight answer is exactly what's needed. When that's the case it's often best to switch to a different system of divination (more on those later). And sometimes rephrasing your question to make it more open ended is all you need to let tarot give you the best answer possible.

## Yes/No in Tarot: The Methods

* **Upright vs. reversed.** Upright means yes, reversed means no. Card orientation comes from how you shuffled, not from anything the deck is telling you. It can also create a bit of a mixed answer when a card like The Sun appears reversed, or the Ten of Swords appears upright.
* **Assigned meanings.** Even numbers yes, odd numbers no, courts maybe. Or Cups and Wands for yes, Swords and Pentacles for no, Majors for unclear. No two sources assign the same way. This method can work well for those using tarot for spirit communication.
* **Favorable vs. unfavorable.** Sort the cards into pleasant and unpleasant, then read pleasant as yes. This one at least uses card meaning, but it confuses "is this good" with "will this happen."

## What a Tarot Card Does Instead

A tarot card describes a situation. It doesn't deliver a verdict. Reduce a card to yes or no and you keep one word out of everything it was telling you.

Say you're waiting to hear about a job. A yes/no method gives you "yes." Read the same cards normally and they'll show you the offer is coming but the money is worse than you hoped, or that what's bothering you is leaving your current team rather than the job itself.

## Ask a Better Question First

Before reaching for another method, check whether the binary is what you need. "Will I get the job?" hides a few questions better suited to tarot: what you need to know to improve your chances, what you'll run into in the interview, where the job leads in both the good version and the bad one. Each of those gives tarot something to work with. The full guidelines are in [how to ask better tarot questions](https://juniperdivination.com/good-questions-for-tarot-readings/).

When the binary is really a choice between two options, the [quick decision spread](https://juniperdivination.com/free-tarot-spreads/quick-decision-tarot-spread) lays both out side by side and shows you what you're missing about each.

## Other Divination Methods for Yes/No Questions

Sometimes the question really is binary. These methods are built for it.

- **Geomancy.** My top recommendation. Geomancy was made for direct questions, and every reading ends in a single figure that gives you the answer. More on it below.
- **Pendulums.** Hold the weight still, ask, and read the swing. Most readers assign one direction to yes and the other to no, calibrating with questions they already know the answers to. It's the fastest of these to learn, but some people find it hard not to influence the response.
- **Coin tossing.** Heads yes, tails no. Many readers keep one coin or flat object for divination only. For more in this range, the guide to [divination without buying anything](https://juniperdivination.com/divination-without-buying-anything) covers several.

## Geomancy: A System Built for Direct Questions

Geomancy comes from the Arabic *'ilm al-raml*, "the science of the sand." A reader made rows of dots in sand or dirt, counted them, and built a chart from the result. It reached Europe in the medieval period and stayed in use as a standard divinatory art through the Renaissance.

To start a geomantic chart you make sixteen lines of dots on paper without counting as you go, then count each line and mark if the total number of dots was odd or even. Everything after that is arithmetic. The chart derives itself and ends in one figure called the Judge.

The Judge is your answer. There are sixteen possible figures, and each carries a traditional character: some favorable, some unfavorable, some mixed. So the Judge tells you what kind of yes or what kind of no you're getting. Two supporting figures, the Witnesses, show how the situation reaches that answer. You get a verdict and the reasoning behind it, which is what the yes/no tarot methods above can't produce.

Start with [*The Art and Practice of Geomancy* by John Michael Greer](https://bookshop.org/a/84772/9781578634316). It covers casting, the sixteen figures and their meanings, and the twelve-house chart for detailed questions, with enough worked examples that you can read for yourself before you finish it. You need paper, a pen, and the patience to make sixteen rows of dots.
