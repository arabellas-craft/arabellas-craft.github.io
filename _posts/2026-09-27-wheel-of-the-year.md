The Irish year opened each of its seasons with a festival. By the time anyone wrote them down, they fell on November 1st, February 1st, May 1st and August 1st of the Roman calendar, which reached Ireland along with Christianity and writing.[^1] How the festivals were timed before that isn't known (although they were likely near those dates). The same dates still ran the farming year in the twentieth century, when folklorists wrote down Irish customs.[^2] The four festivals are the calendar [Celtic Reconstructionism](/celtic-reconstructionism-introduction-guide) keeps.

Irish communities also lit bonfires at midsummer and made Christmas the high point of winter.[^1] The Wiccan Wheel of the Year adds both solstices and both equinoxes to the four festivals to make eight. It was put together in England in the 1950s, from sources that weren't Irish.

## The four Gaelic festivals

The medieval tale *Tochmarc Emire*, "The Wooing of Emer," names all four festivals in a version written down in the eleventh or twelfth century, and gives a brief description of each.[^3]

**Samhain** (pronounced [SAH-win](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/Samhain){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}), November 1st, is "Samhain, when the summer goes to its rest."[^1] It opened winter and closed the farmer's year: the harvest was in, and the cattle came down from the summer pastures to the fields near the house.[^2] See [What is Samhain?](/what-is-samhain) and [Irish Halloween traditions](/samhain-folk-customs).

**Imbolc** (pronounced IM-olk), February 1st, is "Imbolc, when the ewes are milked at spring's beginning."[^1] That's the medieval name. In the folk record the day is St. Brigid's Day, Lá Fhéile Bríde (pronounced [law AY-luh BREE-juh](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/L%C3%A1_Fh%C3%A9ile_Br%C3%ADde){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}), the first day of spring and of the farmer's working year.[^2] See [preparing the home for Imbolc](/st-brigids-day-preparing-home) and [St. Brigid's crosses](/st-brigids-day-crosses).

**Bealtaine** (pronounced [BYAL-tin-uh](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/Bealtaine){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}), May 1st, is "Beltine at the summer's beginning."[^1] It was a gale day, when half a year's rent fell due, and hiring fairs were held where farm workers found their next place. The cattle went out to the summer grazing.[^2] See [Irish folk customs for Bealtaine](/irish-bealtaine-traditions) and [the history of modern Beltane traditions](/history-of-modern-beltane-traditions).

**Lúnasa** (pronounced [LOO-nuh-suh](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/L%C3%BAnasa){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}), August 1st, opened autumn and the harvest. *Tochmarc Emire* calls it "\[Brón\] Trogain, earth's sorrowing in autumn," and other texts just as old call it Lughnasadh.[^1] The gatherings later moved to a nearby Sunday, usually the last one in July.[^2] See [What is Lughnasadh?](/what-is-lughnasadh), its [pre-Christian origins](/lunasa-pre-christian-origins) and its [folk traditions](/lunasa-folk-traditions).

## Why the Irish seasons start in November, February, May, and August

Each festival is the first day of a season, so the solstices and equinoxes land mid-season. You can see this in the Irish names for months: September is Meán Fómhair, "mid-autumn."[^4]

*Tochmarc Emire* divides the year into "the summer from Beltine to Samain, and the winter from Samain to Beltine": the months the cattle were out on the hills, and the months they were home.[^3] Marie-Louise Sjoestedt, writing in 1940, called it a calendar "regulated not by the solar year, by solstice and equinox, but by the agrarian and pastoral year."[^5] Ronald Hutton adds that medieval Irish documents start the year at Christmas, January 1st or March 25th, so the Irish "operated a dual system, of reckoning time both from midwinter and from the opening of seasons."[^1]

You'll also see the four festivals called "cross-quarter days." Modern pagan writers count the solstices and equinoxes as the quarters and the four festivals as the "cross-quarter" days between them.[^6] In the Irish calendar the four festivals are the quarter days themselves.[^1]

## What Ireland did at the solstices and equinoxes

**Midsummer** was one of the biggest nights of the Irish year. Bonfires were lit on St. John's Eve, June 23rd, and Kevin Danaher records communal midsummer fires in every county.[^2] A Galway school collection from the 1930s:

> On St. John's night, June 23rd, there are bonfires lit all over the place. Each village has its own bonfire.[^7]

The custom wasn't only Irish: Hutton finds the same bonfires across England, Wales and Lowland Scotland, "with complete irrelevance to boundaries between Celtic areas and others."[^1]

**Midwinter**: at the winter solstice the rising sun shines through an opening above the entrance of Newgrange, a passage tomb built before 3000 BCE, and down the passage into its inner chamber.[^1][^8] Archaeologist Michael O'Kelly first saw it there in 1967, after local people told him the sun used to reach the chamber. He had rebuilt the opening during the excavation, and how closely it matches the original is debated.[^8]

The folk calendar's midwinter was Christmas, the most important of the Christian feasts to Irish people, running to Little Christmas on January 6th. Irish speakers called that Nollaig na mBan, "Women's Christmas."[^2] A medieval Irish text has the high king Brian Boru holding court "from great Christmas to little Christmas."[^1] [Lá Coille](/irish-new-year-folk-magic-la-coille) covers the New Year customs in between.

**The equinoxes** had no Irish festival of their own. Lady Day, March 25th, was a Church holy day and, until 1752, the start of the legal year, but Danaher says "it had little effect on popular tradition."[^2] Michaelmas, September 29th, came in with Anglo-Norman law.[^2] Across northern Europe, Hutton finds no festival recorded at the spring equinox before Easter, and the autumn one "even less evident."[^9]

## Where the eight-festival Wheel of the Year came from

The Wheel of the Year joins two sets of dates from two different sources, which Ronald Hutton traced in a 2008 study.[^9]

The solstices and equinoxes came from Edward Williams, the Welsh writer known as Iolo Morganwg. Between 1792 and 1826 he invented a system of Druid festivals at the solstices and equinoxes as part of a Druid tradition he presented as ancient.[^9]

The four seasonal festivals came from Margaret Murray. Her 1921 book *The Witch-Cult in Western Europe* argued that people tried as witches had kept a surviving pagan religion, with four great sabbats at Candlemas, May Day, Lammas and All-Hallows.[^9][^10] Historians now reject it.[^9]

Gerald Gardner, who made Wicca public in the 1950s, built on Murray's work. His coven added the solstices by 1953 and the equinoxes soon after, as lesser feasts. In early 1958 the coven asked for all eight to count equally, and Gardner agreed.[^9] His witches still used the English names in 1959, though he compared their festivals to Samhain, Beltane and Lughnasadh.[^11] Later, American Wiccans took up the Gaelic names themselves.[^6]

The solstices and equinoxes got their names in California. Aidan Kelly called the summer solstice Litha, a month name recorded by the Anglo-Saxon monk Bede, and the spring equinox Ostara, an early Germanic name for Easter that the folklorist Jacob Grimm made famous.[^9] He named the autumn equinox Mabon, after Mabon son of Modron, a character in medieval Welsh stories. Kelly dates the names to a pagan calendar he made in 1974, and they spread through the magazine *Green Egg*.[^12] The phrase "the wheel of the Celtic year" was in print by 1989.[^9]

How CR came to keep only the Irish four is in the [history of Celtic Reconstructionism](/celtic-reconstructionism-history).

## Keeping the Irish calendar today

To follow the Irish year, keep the four festivals, with St. John's Eve as a fifth and Christmas for midwinter. If you already keep the Wheel of the Year, the Irish customs fit straight in, and there are [seasonal tarot spreads](/free-tarot-spreads/seasonal/) for each point of the year.

## Common questions

**Is the Wheel of the Year Celtic?**

Half of it matches the Irish calendar, though Wicca took those four from Margaret Murray, not from Irish sources. The solstices and equinoxes came from Iolo Morganwg's Druid calendar.[^9]

**Is Mabon a Celtic holiday?**

No. Mabon is a figure in medieval Welsh stories, and Aidan Kelly gave the name to the autumn equinox in the 1970s.[^9]

**What are the Irish pagan holidays?**

Samhain, Imbolc, Bealtaine and Lúnasa, the four festivals the medieval texts name. The festivals likely predate Christianity in Ireland, but the customs people wrote down in the last three centuries were kept by Christian communities, often on a saint's day.[^1]

**Are they "Celtic fire festivals"?**

Of the four, only Bealtaine had a strong fire custom in Ireland. The biggest Irish bonfires were at midsummer. The label comes from a Victorian folklorist's misreading of a medieval Irish glossary.[^1]

**What are the cross-quarter days?**

The four festivals, counted from the solstices and equinoxes: around February 1st, May 1st, August 1st and November 1st.

**Was Samhain the Celtic New Year?**

There's no firm evidence for it in the medieval sources, which start the year at Christmas, January 1st or March 25th.[^1][^3] [Five claims about Samhain](/halloween-origins-samhain-claims) traces where the idea came from.
