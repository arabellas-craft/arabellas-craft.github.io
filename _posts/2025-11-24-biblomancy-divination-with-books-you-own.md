About 1,900 years ago, a Roman soldier and politician named Hadrian was anxious about where he stood with the reigning emperor, Trajan. So he opened a copy of Vergil, Rome's best-known poet, at random and read the lines in front of him.[^1] Bibliomancy is the same practice today. You ask a question, open a book to a random page, and read the passage you land on as your answer.

You don't need a deck or anything else you'd have to buy. If you own books, you can start tonight. It's one of the methods in my [beginner's guide to divination methods](/beginner-divination-methods-guide), and it's the easiest one to try.

## What Is Bibliomancy?

The word *bibliomancy* joins the Greek word for book with *-mancy*, the ending used for kinds of divination, as in cartomancy. Someone who practices it is a bibliomancer.

The method stays the same whatever book you use. Hold a question in mind, let chance pick the page, and read what you find as if it were written to you.

A random passage feels relevant because good writing is dense. One sentence about a storm or a locked door can be read a dozen ways. When you come to it with a question, your mind looks for the reading that fits your situation, and it usually finds one.

The passage works as a prompt. It hands you words you didn't choose, so you think about your problem from an angle you wouldn't have picked yourself. Many people find it brings up something they already suspected but hadn't said out loud.

Some practitioners see a god or spirit guiding the page they land on. Others treat it purely as a thinking tool. The method works the same either way.

## A Short History of Book Divination

### Homer and Vergil

The Greeks and Romans used their great poets this way. It became customary to open Homer or Vergil at random and take the first lines you saw as the answer.[^2] The Latin name for the Vergil version is *sortes Vergilianae*, the Vergilian lots.

Hadrian's reading is the most famous. He landed on *Aeneid* 6.808–812 and took it as a sign that Trajan favored him and would name him heir.[^2] Trajan did, and Hadrian became emperor. A later emperor, Alexander Severus, consulted Vergil too and read his lines as a promise that he would rule.[^2]

The habit outlasted Rome. One scholar counted 169 recorded uses of the Vergilian lots in the 1500s alone. King Charles I of England tried it at Oxford and landed on Dido's curse from *Aeneid* Book 4. A study published in 1922 found people still consulting Vergil this way.[^2]

### The Bible

Christians turned to scripture. The *sortes Biblicae*, the Bible lots, are recorded from the early 300s. Into the Carolingian era, people used them for serious church business, including the election of bishops.[^2]

The best-known case is Augustine of Hippo. In his *Confessions*, he describes being stuck, unable to commit to the change he wanted to make in his life. He heard a child's voice nearby chanting *tolle lege*, "take up and read." He remembered that Saint Antony had once heard a Gospel line read aloud and taken it as a message meant for him. So Augustine picked up his copy of Paul's letters, opened it, and read the first passage his eyes fell on: Romans 13:13–14.[^3] That reading settled his conversion.

Augustine grew wary of the practice later on. In a letter, he wrote that people who tell the future from random Gospel pages do better than those who consult spirits, but that using scripture for worldly affairs is still wrong.[^4] A separate collection called the *Sortes Sanctorum* was repeatedly condemned by medieval councils. Bible lots faded after the Carolingian era but never fully stopped.[^2]

### Hafez in Iran

Book divination is still everyday practice in Iran. Most Iranian homes keep a copy of the *Divan*, the collected poems of Hafez. At Nowruz, the Persian New Year, and at the winter solstice festival of Yalda, people open it to a random page and read the poem as guidance. Readers for hire work outside his tomb in Shiraz, opening the book for visitors and explaining the poem's meaning.[^5] The practice is called *fal-e Hafez*, a Hafez reading.

## How to Do Bibliomancy

1. **Pick your book.** More on choosing one below. For a first try, grab a book of poetry or a novel you love.
2. **Write down your question.** Open questions work best. "What do I need to understand about this job offer?" gives the passage room to say something. "Should I take the job?" asks for a yes or no, and a paragraph about a garden can't really give you one.
3. **Hold the closed book and think about your question.** Take a few slow breaths. Keep the question in mind until it feels settled.
4. **Let chance pick the page.** Use one of these and stick with it:
   - **Blind finger.** Close your eyes and flip through the pages. Stop when it feels right, let the book fall open, and put your finger on the page without looking.
   - **Numbers.** Roll dice or use a random number generator to pick a page number, then a line number. Turn to that page and count down to that line.
5. **Read the passage.** Start with the full sentence your finger or line number landed on. Then read two or three sentences on either side for context. With a poem, read the whole stanza.
6. **Write it all down.** Record the date, your question, the book, the page, and the passage itself. Then write your first reaction before you start analyzing it.

## Best Books for Bibliomancy

Any book works, but some give better readings than others. You want language that can mean more than one thing.

- **Poetry.** Short poems full of images are open to many readings. Collections where each poem stands on its own, like Emily Dickinson or Hafez in translation, are easy to read from a random page.
- **Novels you know well.** You'll recognize the scene you land in, which adds context. Novels with rich, descriptive prose give you more to work with than fast plot-driven ones.
- **Philosophy and essays.** These suit questions about decisions and values. Marcus Aurelius' *Meditations* works well because each section is a short, complete thought.
- **Sacred texts.** The Bible, the *Tao Te Ching*, or whatever text matters to you. This is the oldest form of the practice.
- **Your own old journals.** Your past self was writing about a different problem, but the words can land surprisingly close to your current one.

Textbooks, manuals, and cookbooks are harder to read this way. Their sentences are written to mean one thing, so there's little to interpret.

**One book or a whole shelf?** Using the same book over and over helps you learn its voice. You start to notice which themes keep coming up for you. Pulling a random book off the shelf first adds another layer of chance, which some people enjoy.

## How to Read Your Passage

**Trust your first reaction.** What you feel in the first few seconds, before you start overthinking, usually holds the core of the message. Notice which words catch your eye.

**Look past the literal meaning.** A passage about crossing water might mean travel. It might also point to a risk, a change, or leaving something behind. Ask what the passage is about underneath its surface details.

**Let one word do the work.** Sometimes a single word or phrase is the whole reading, and the rest of the passage is context.

**Treat confusion as information.** Some passages make no sense at first. Write them down anyway. The meaning often shows up days later.

**Go back to your notes.** Re-read old readings after a week or a month. You'll spot patterns, and you'll see which readings turned out to fit. Your skill at reading passages grows with practice.

Bibliomancy also pairs well with other methods. You can [combine it with playing cards](/combine-tarot-divination-methods), using the cards to show what's happening and a passage to show how to think about it.

## Bibliomancy Online

You can do this with e-books too. Use a random number generator to pick a page or a percentage, jump there, and read the first full sentence. Project Gutenberg has thousands of free public-domain books, so you can try Vergil, the Bible, or Dickinson without buying anything. Some sites offer a digital *fal-e Hafez* that picks a poem for you with one click.[^6] The method stays the same: ask your question first, then let chance pick.
