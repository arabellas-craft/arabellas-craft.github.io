Samhain (pronounced [SAH-win](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/Samhain){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}) is the Irish festival of the first day of winter, kept on November 1st. The celebrating starts the evening before, on Oíche Shamhna (pronounced [EE-huh HOW-nuh](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/O%C3%ADche_Shamhna){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}), October 31st.[^1] In 2026 that puts Samhain on Sunday November 1st and Oíche Shamhna on Saturday October 31st. In modern Irish the word is the name of the month as well: Mí na Samhna is November, Lá Samhna is November 1st, and Oíche Shamhna is what English calls Halloween.[^2] It's one of the four quarter days of the Irish year, with Imbolc, Bealtaine and Lúnasa.[^3]

You'll also read that Samhain was the Celtic New Year, that the veil between worlds thinned that night, and that the Church put All Saints' Day on November 1st to cover it up. Each of those has a history, and it's rarely the one given: [five claims about Samhain and where they come from](/halloween-origins-samhain-claims) traces them. This article stays with the plainer questions, and the [folk customs](/samhain-folk-customs) and [divination games](/samhain-divination-games) have articles of their own.

## How to pronounce Samhain

Two syllables, stress on the first, and the *mh* is a w sound: SAH-win. *Sam-hane*, the spelling pronunciation, isn't one of the options. Teanglann's pronunciation database has native speakers saying the word in the Ulster, Connacht and Munster dialects, and the vowels shift between them, so [hear all three](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/Samhain){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.[^4] Oíche Shamhna has [its own recordings](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/O%C3%ADche_Shamhna){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} there.

## What the name means

Everyday modern Irish uses *Samhain* for November, and Ó Dónaill's dictionary gives the festival as a second, literary sense of the same word.[^2] The older spelling you'll meet in medieval texts is *Samain*, and those texts gloss it for you. *Tochmarc Emire*, "The Wooing of Emer," says: "Samain, i.e., summer end... the summer from Beltine to Samain, and the winter from Samain to Beltine."[^5]

Modern philologists are cool on that gloss. The Celticist Lisa Spangenberg notes that the derivation from Old Irish *sam*, "summer," plus *fuin*, "end," has a problem in its second half: the suffix is "somewhat unlikely, philologically and morphologically speaking, though it is a persuasive folk etymology, even one used in the medieval glosses." Whitley Stokes proposed a different root in 1907, proto-Celtic *\*samani*, "assembly," and in 1959 J. Vendryes concluded that the summer words are unrelated to *samain* altogether.[^7] The meaning everyone repeats is the one the medieval scribes liked best and the one the linguists trust least.

## When Samhain falls, and why the eve carries the weight

The date is fixed: November 1st, every year, with no moon phase or equinox involved. Kevin Danaher's survey of the Irish calendar year puts it plainly: "The ancient festival of Samhain, the first day of Winter is traditionally kept on 1 November, the Feast of All Saints, and the vigil of this day, Oíche Shamhna, Hallow E'en, Hollantide, is celebrated all over Ireland with feasting, merrymaking and divination."[^1]

Almost everything happened on the eve rather than the day, which follows the Irish reckoning of time: Dáithí Ó hÓgáin sums it up as "the Celtic belief that the night comes before the day."[^6]

## Samhain and Halloween

Samhain is the Irish name for November 1st and for the festival on it. Halloween is All Hallows' Eve, the vigil of All Saints' Day, which the Western church also keeps on November 1st. The two land on the same night, which in Ireland collected names from both directions: Danaher records Oíche Shamhna and Hollantide alongside Oíche na Sprideanna (spirit night), Púca Night, Snap-Apple Night, Nut-crack Night and Oíche na n-úll, apple night.[^1]

Which came first, and how much of the modern holiday came out of Ireland, is a longer argument with a real evidence trail behind it: that's [the five-claims article](/halloween-origins-samhain-claims).

## What the day marked

Samhain closed the farming year. Danaher describes it as the point when everything had to be secure for the hard season: the crops harvested and stored, dry cattle and sheep brought down from the mountain pastures, milking cows moved into the byre and put on stored fodder.[^1] It was the settling day too. "Everyone has debts at Hallow E'en," runs the proverb Danaher quotes; wages, rents and the hire of grazing and tillage were all squared up.[^1]

In the medieval tales the same date is where the big gatherings happen: Ronald Hutton calls it "an ideal moment for the convention of the year's most important tribal assemblies," and the *feis* of Samhain is a favourite setting in the early Irish stories.[^8]

What people did on the night, from the colcannon to the ring in the brack, is in the [folk customs of Samhain](/samhain-folk-customs). If you mark it with a reading, there's a [Samhain tarot spread](/free-tarot-spreads/samhain-tarot-spread) here too.
