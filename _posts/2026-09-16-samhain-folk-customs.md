Oíche Shamhna (pronounced [EE-huh HOW-nuh](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/O%C3%ADche_Shamhna){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}), the eve of [Samhain](/what-is-samhain) on October 31st, was a night of food and games at home. The fairies and the souls of the dead were thought to be out, and people dealt with them by following certain rules and customs.

Irish schoolchildren wrote these customs down for the Dúchas Schools' Collection in the 1930s, from what their parents and grandparents told them. The fortune-telling games of the same night have [their own article](/samhain-divination-games).

## The meal

Hallow Eve was the vigil of All Saints, a day when no meat was eaten.[^1]

Even the poorest house had something special for supper.[^1] It was built on potatoes, and the dish depended on where you lived. Colcannon, mashed potato with cabbage and onion,[^1] was most common in Leinster.[^2] Boxty, a cake of grated raw potato mixed with mashed potato, was most common in Cavan, Leitrim and Donegal.[^3] Donegal also had poundies or brúitín, mashed potatoes eaten out of one dish with a lump of butter in the middle.[^4] Some houses left a plate of colcannon on the table for the fairies, with spoons stuck in it.[^5]

Apples and nuts came after the meal, along with a brack with [a ring in it](/samhain-divination-games), often bought from a shop.

## Snap-apple

The simplest version was an apple hung from the ceiling on a string. Players tied their hands behind their backs and tried to bite it.

In Munster and south Leinster the apple often went on a wooden cross hung from the ceiling, with lighted candles on the other arms. The cross was spun, so a player could just as easily catch a candle.[^6] Some houses added a bar of soap or a potato. In Munster these apple games were often called snap-apple.[^7]

## Bobbing for apples

A tub or basin of water went on the floor with apples floating in it. Players knelt beside it with their hands behind their backs and tried to catch an apple with their mouths. Many houses dropped a coin to the bottom as well, and whoever brought it up kept it.

## Guising

Young people, mostly boys, dressed in old clothes with false faces or soot on their faces and went from house to house singing and dancing. Houses gave them apples, nuts, a slice of brack or a few pennies. In Cavan they asked for boxty, and took it if they were refused.[^8]

Guisers were called mummers in Monaghan and pucas in Kildare, and in Wexford the custom was called vizarding.[^9]

Where the custom came from is in [the article on Samhain's popular claims](/halloween-origins-samhain-claims).

## Pranks

- Pull cabbages from a neighbor's garden and throw them at doors or beat the doors with them. It was most common in the west and northwest.[^10]
- Take gates off their hinges and hide them, sometimes miles away.
- Tie doors shut from outside, or tie two neighbors' door knockers together.
- Stuff a chimney with hay or cover it with a wet bag to fill the house with smoke.
- Rig a door so whoever opens it gets hit: a bucket of water or a turnip tied to the latch, or a brush dressed as a man leaning against it.
- Hollow out a turnip, cut a face in it, put a lit candle inside, and set it on a wall to frighten people.[^11] A Leitrim account does it with a pumpkin, which "is something like a turnip."[^12]

## Water after dark

People avoided throwing water out of the house that night. The usual reason was the poor souls, who were about and might get it thrown on them.[^13] In Galway and Mayo it was the fairies, and a fairy hit with water could take revenge.[^14] If you had to throw water out, you called a warning first, *Seachain!* ("beware!") or *chugaibh an t-uisce!* ("water towards you!"), so they could step out of the way.[^1]

## The door on the latch

Before bed, people swept the hearth, built up the fire, put chairs around it and left water and food on the table. The door was left on the latch.[^15] This was for the souls of the dead, who were thought to come home that night.[^16] In some places the food was left for the fairies instead.[^17] Many people did all this on All Souls' Night rather than Hallow Eve, since that was the night most expected the dead to come home.[^1]

## A candle in the window

Candles for the dead were mostly lit on All Souls' Night,[^18] often one for each dead member of the family.[^1] Many were set in a window, where they were said to light the souls' way to Heaven.[^19] In parts of Munster the candles were lit on Hallow Eve itself.[^20]

## Staying in

People didn't go out late that night, and especially not after midnight. Depending on the district, the danger was the fairies, the púca, or the souls of the dead, which were said to be as thick as blades of grass.[^21]

A late traveler carried a black-handled knife or had a steel needle stuck in his coat collar or sleeve. If the fairies led him astray, he could turn his coat inside out so they wouldn't know him.[^1]

## The blackberries

Blackberries weren't eaten after Hallow Eve, and in many places haws and sloes weren't either. Some accounts say the púca spat on them or spoiled them that night, and others say it was the fairies.[^22]

## These customs in modern practice

Most of these customs are still very accessible. Make colcannon or boxty for dinner, and set the first plate aside if you want to. Hang an apple on a string, or fill a basin for bobbing. Leave a light in the window or a glass of water on the table for the people you've lost. And leave the blackberries on the bush after the 31st.
