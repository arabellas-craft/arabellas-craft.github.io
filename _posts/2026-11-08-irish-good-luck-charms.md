In Ireland in the 1930s, good luck charms were things you found on the road, handed over at a fair, or carried in your pocket. Schoolchildren wrote about them for the Dúchas Schools' Collection between 1937 and 1939, taking down what their parents and grandparents told them.

When folklorists say "charm," they usually mean words: something spoken or written to cure an illness or protect someone.[^1] The charms here are objects. Horseshoes and luck pennies turn up in accounts from all over the country, and so does the four-leaved shamrock. The Claddagh ring, sold today as a lucky charm, never shows up as one.

## Horseshoes

"Irishmen show great respect to horse-shoes and why they do so we do not know," one Galway account begins.[^2]

Folklorists still don't agree on why, either. The luck might come from the iron, since people believed iron drove off spirits. It might come from the shape: the two points look like the horns of a crescent moon, and some writers saw a coiled snake in the curve. Or it might come from the horse, which some people saw as sacred, or from the smith who made the shoe.[^3]

The only reason the Dúchas accounts give involves a smith. In a Limerick story, a smith heated a horseshoe red in the fire, faced the devil with it, and made him promise "that he would never enter a house where there is a horseshoe nailed on the door."[^4]

Accounts didn't agree on which way up the horseshoe went either. From Mayo:

> Most people turn the horse shoe upside down. That is wrong. When you are nailing the horse shoe to the door do not turn it upside down if you do the luck will fall out.[^5]

A Cork account says to nail them "with the points uppermost so as to prevent the luck falling,"[^6] and in Donegal, "It is unlucky to hang up a horse-shoe with the heels down."[^7] But in Dublin, the horseshoe over the cow-shed door "points downwards."[^8]

Horseshoes didn't only go on doors. In Monaghan, people put one near a hatching hen "to keep thunder from killing the birds."[^9] In Mayo, "When you are building a new house it is very lucky to put a donkey's shoe in the foundation."[^10]

## Luck Found on the Road

In Galway, a man who found a horseshoe on the road would "bring it home and hang it over the door of his kitchen."[^2] In Mayo, a found shoe brought "all the more luck if the shoe is studded with nails."[^11]

Most lucky finds were bits of metal. One Monaghan account lists "farthings, horse nails and horseshoes." Another lists "a half-penny, a horse shoe, a nail or a pin or any piece of iron."[^12] In Cavan, a pin was lucky if it lay with "the point of it towards you," and you'd "lift it and carry it a piece of the road."[^18] Ó Súilleabháin's handbook for folklore collectors files the "belief that it is lucky to find a piece of an old plough" under iron, too.[^13]

But a find could also carry someone else's trouble. People cured warts by rubbing them on stones, then leaving the stones where someone else would pick them up.[^14] On May Day, Danaher writes, you didn't pick up anything you found, because someone setting a charm could use it "to gain power over the finder."[^15]

So people were careful with what they found, and spitting was the usual protection. In Cork, you'd "pick it up spit on it & throw it over your left shoulder for luck."[^16] In Dublin, you'd spit on a found piece of iron "and throw it away."[^17]

## The Luck Penny

When someone sold an animal at a fair, they handed part of the price back to the buyer. That was the luck penny.

It was rarely a penny. At Gallen Fair in Offaly, "If he sells for £1 he gives 1/- (one shilling). If he gets £5 he gives 2/6 luck (two shillings & six pence)."[^19] Kilkenny people still called it a luck penny, though, "because a penny is the luckiest."[^20]

In Meath, "If 'luck penny' was not given the animal would not thrive."[^21] In Cork, both people spat on the coin, and the buyer kept it "in some safe place in the house until the animal etc is sold again." Spend it, and "he is after parting with his luck," meaning he'd just thrown it away.[^22]

In Cavan, dealers sometimes bought "clear to pocket," which meant no luck penny at all.[^23] Leitrim had it the other way around: the buyer gave the luck penny to the seller.[^24]

Handsel Monday, the first Monday of the year, ran on similar logic: the first money that changed hands set the luck for what followed. It's covered in [Irish New Year customs](/irish-new-year-folk-magic-la-coille).

## The Four-Leaved Shamrock

In Irish folklore, a four-leaved shamrock let you see through a spell. From a Longford account: "If you have a four-leafed shamrock about you, without knowing it, you'll know if there's trickery going on."[^25]

The luck also depended on not trying. In Kerry, it was lucky "to find a four-leaved shamrock without looking for it. We should not reveal the secret of finding it otherwise our luck would be interfered with."[^27] In Galway, finding one meant seven years of luck.[^28]

Seven years comes up in Cavan too, where "a four-leafed shamrock will grow for seven years in a place where a mare foals."[^18] In Monaghan, the mare's owners dug it up, and it was "planted and kept for good luck."[^26]

The belief isn't only Irish. A book printed in London in 1620 already tied four-leaved grass to luck.[^29]

## Things People Kept or Carried

- **A caul**, a piece of membrane some babies are born with over the face. In Cork, the father kept it in his purse, and it "was believed to bring great luck to the parents and the child."[^30] In Laois, a family cut off pieces for emigrants sailing to America on the "coffin ships," the crowded ships where many passengers died on the way.[^31] In Kerry, ship captains paid for them "because it is supposed to save the ship from every danger."[^32]
- **A stoat-skin purse.** Danaher records that it "was very lucky, for it was never empty of money."[^33]
- **Something for luck at cards.** A rabbit's foot in your pocket in Mayo,[^34] a crooked pin stuck in a player's coat in Kildare,[^35] or, also in Kildare, a piece of iron put in the fire "to bring luck by the power of the devil."[^36]
- **A lucky coin.** In Louth, Biddy Rice's halfpenny "always came back to her again," however often she spent it, and "She was called lucky Biddy on that very account."[^37]

May flowers and branches kept in the house for luck are in [Bealtaine customs](/irish-bealtaine-traditions).

## The Symbols Sold Today

The **shamrock** is Ireland's best-known symbol. The earliest record of people wearing it is from 1681, and the story of St Patrick using it to explain the Trinity was first written down in the early 1700s.[^38] Most Dúchas pages about it are about [St Patrick's Day](/st-patricks-day-irish-customs-folklore), but a few use it for luck. An Offaly account says, "A bunch of shamrock is usually hung in a stable for luck,"[^39] and in Cork, St Patrick's Day shamrock was fastened into the horseshoes over the outhouse doors.[^6]

The **Claddagh ring** comes from Galway: two hands holding a crowned heart. Its modern form was first made in the 1600s.[^40] It shows up once in Dúchas: the King of Claddagh "wore the Claddagh Ring" at his wedding, "and when he died he left it to the next king."[^41] Nothing there ties it to luck.

Celtic **knotwork** reached Ireland by the 7th century,[^42] but nothing in Dúchas ties any knot to luck.

The **leprechaun** in the oldest story, *Echtra Fergusa maic Léti*, carries a king off to the sea and grants him wishes in return for being let go.[^43] Dúchas ties it to luck directly: one Mayo man was said to have kept a leprechaun in a box for twenty-one years, "as he heard it was very lucky to have a leprechaun."[^44]

## The Luck of the Irish

The phrase "the luck of the Irish" doesn't turn up in the Schools' Collection.

When older writers put luck and the Irish together, it was usually bad luck. An Irish journal from 1857 wrote of "the general ill luck of the Irish in battle on their own soil."[^46] In 1892, a Sydney paper said it was "the ill luck of the Irish" to meet the Swedes early in a tug-of-war.[^47]

Historian Edward T. O'Donnell ties the good-luck version to the gold and silver rushes of the late 1800s, when some of the most successful miners were Irish or Irish American. He says the phrase carried "a certain tone of derision": the Irish could only have struck it rich by luck.[^45]

The phrase spread in the 1900s. Harold MacGrath's novel *The Luck of the Irish* came out in 1917 and became a film in 1920.[^48] A second film of that name, with Tyrone Power and a leprechaun, followed in 1948.[^49]

## Found, Handed Over or Carried

In these accounts, luck came to you. You found it on the road or got it back at a fair. What mattered was what you did next: which way you nailed up the shoe, and whether you kept the luck penny or spent it.

The symbols sold as Irish luck today barely show up. The shamrock belonged to St Patrick's Day, the Claddagh ring was never called lucky, and no knot was known for bringing luck. The leprechaun comes closest, but you'd have to catch one first.
