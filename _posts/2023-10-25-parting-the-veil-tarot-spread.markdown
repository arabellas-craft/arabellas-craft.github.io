Parting the Veil is a six-card spread for sitting with someone who died. One card for who they were, three for what you'd hear from them, one for what they'd tell you to do, and one for where you can set it down.

Whether you read the cards as contact or as your own mind finally getting a word in, the spread does the same work. It gives an unfinished conversation somewhere to go.

## When to Use This Spread

Samhain and Halloween are the obvious time, and that's the season this spread was built for. It works any time of year.

Use it when there's something you never got to say, or never got to hear. An argument that never resolved. A death that came too fast for goodbyes. A person whose approval you're still working for years later.

It also works for the distant dead. A grandparent you barely met, a relative you know only through stories, someone whose choices shaped your family before you were in it.

Skip it in fresh grief. In the first weeks the cards will mostly hand your own rawness back to you, and there's no benefit in that. Give it time. And if a reading leaves you worse rather than steadier, stop and talk to someone who knew them, or to a grief counsellor. A spread is a good place to think and a poor substitute for people.

## The Card Positions

**Card 1: The Spirit.**

A card for the person. Not how they died, not how you feel about them: who they were.

Read this one as a character sketch. The Emperor for someone who ran things and expected to be obeyed. The Three of Cups for the one who made every room louder. The Hermit for a person who kept their own counsel and left you guessing.

Expect it to be unflattering sometimes. People are difficult, and the dead don't stop being difficult once they're dead. A card that names something you didn't like about them is doing its job, and it usually makes the rest of the reading land harder.

**Cards 2, 3 and 4: The Message.**

Three cards, read together rather than one at a time. This is what you'd hear from them if you could.

Three cards is deliberate. One card gives you a fortune-cookie line you can bend to whatever you already believed. Three cards have to be reconciled with each other, which is harder and more useful.

Lay them left to right and look for the tension between them first. Regret sitting next to pride. Warmth next to a warning. That friction is usually where the real message is, because real people don't say one clean thing.

If all three read as a single obvious statement, take that seriously rather than digging for something more complicated.

**Card 5: The Advice.**

What they'd tell you to do about it.

This is the position where the reading turns toward you and your life now. Cards 2 through 4 are about them. This one is about what you carry out of the room.

Be wary of reading advice you already wanted permission for. The clearest sign this card is working is when it tells you something mildly annoying: rest, apologize, stop rehearsing the argument, call your mother.

**Card 6: The Closure.**

What can be set down, by you or by them.

Closure here isn't the same as feeling better. Sometimes the card names something you get to release. Sometimes it names the part that stays, and closure means stopping the fight against it.

The Death card in this position often reads gently, as an ending you're allowed to let be finished. The Four of Swords points at rest you've been refusing. The Ten of Swords sometimes says the worst of it is already behind you, whatever it currently feels like.

## Reading the Spread

Deal all six before you read any of them.

Start with Card 1 alone and get the person clearly in mind. The rest of the spread is easier to read once you know whose voice you're listening for.

Then read 2, 3 and 4 as a group, out loud if you can. Speaking a message aloud tends to expose the parts you're softening.

Read 5 and 6 last, and read them against Card 1. Advice from the person that Card 1 described is more specific, and often more pointed, than advice in the abstract.

## Where the Veil Idea Comes From

The idea that the barrier between worlds thins at Samhain first turns up in print in 1940, and it isn't in the medieval Irish sources, where the divide is permanent and the traffic across it goes both ways all year. ## Tips

Say who you're reading for before you shuffle. Out loud, using their name. It sharpens the reading considerably compared to a vague intention to reach "the ancestors."

One person per reading. Six cards can hold one relationship. They can't hold four grandparents at once.

Write it down, and date it. These readings change meaning when you find them again in a few years, more than most.

Any deck works. A worn deck, a cheap deck, a deck of ordinary playing cards ([how to read those](https://ko-fi.com/s/95f9c29692){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}).
