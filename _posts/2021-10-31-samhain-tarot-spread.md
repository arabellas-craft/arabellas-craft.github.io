Samhain is the first day of winter in the Irish calendar. It falls on November 1st, and the celebrating starts the night before. The medieval tale *Tochmarc Emire* splits the year in two at Samhain and Bealtaine: "the summer from Beltine to Samain, and the winter from Samain to Beltine" ([more on the festival here](/what-is-samhain)).

On a farm, Samhain was a deadline. The harvest had to be in, the cattle had to be down off the mountain, and the year's debts had to be paid. This spread asks five questions for that same turn: what's changing, what you can let go of, what to carry forward, what's coming back, and what will hold you up until spring.

## When to Use This Spread

Oíche Shamhna (October 31st) or November 1st is the obvious time. It also works whenever a busy stretch ends and a quieter or harder one begins: the end of a contract, the week before a move, the first properly cold weekend of the year.

If you want to sit with someone you've lost, [Parting the Veil](/free-tarot-spreads/parting-the-veil-tarot-spread) is built for that. If you'd rather play the old Hallow Eve fortune games, they're [collected here](/samhain-divination-games).

## The Card Positions

**Card 1: What will change in the year to come?**

Read "the year to come" as the twelve months from this Samhain to the next.

People tend to expect a big event in this position. More often the card names something that's already moving, and the reading shows you where it's headed. The Wheel of Fortune points to circumstances shifting around you, more than anything you're steering. The Eight of Wands says the change will come fast. The Tower is the sudden version, and it's easier to take when you've seen it coming.

**Card 2: What can I let go of?**

In Ireland, blackberries weren't eaten after Hallow Eve. Some accounts say the púca spat on them that night, and others blame the fairies ([the custom is here](/samhain-folk-customs)). Whatever was good about them, their season was over.

This card names something like that in your own life. Read it as permission. The Four of Pentacles is something you're gripping harder than it's worth. The Three of Swords is an old hurt you've been carrying around. The Eight of Cups says you've already half-left, and the card is telling you you're allowed to finish.

**Card 3: What wisdom should I take into the next year?**

Samhain was when the harvest had to be stored for winter. This card is the part of your own harvest worth keeping: something you learned this year that you'll need again.

It's often plainer than people expect. The Seven of Pentacles is patience, the lesson of waiting for something to grow. The Hierophant is something you learned from a teacher or a tradition. The Hermit points to something you worked out on your own.

**Card 4: What is ready to be reborn in the new year?**

This is something coming back. It might be an old interest, a friendship that went quiet, or a part of yourself you set aside for a while.

Read it alongside Card 2. One card is what goes and the other is what returns, and they often make more sense as a pair. Judgement is a calling you thought you'd outgrown. The Six of Cups is something from your past. Any Ace says the thing coming back will need starting from scratch.

**Card 5: What will support me through the winter darkness?**

At Samhain the milking cows went into the byre, the cowshed, and onto the fodder stored for winter. This card is your byre: the people, routines and places you'll lean on until the days get longer.

The Four of Wands is home and the people in it. The Ten of Pentacles is family. The Star is hope, which can be enough to get through a hard season on.

## Reading the Spread

Lay Cards 1 and 2 across the top, Cards 3 and 4 across the bottom, and Card 5 in the middle.

Read the top row first. Card 1 is what's changing and Card 2 is what you can drop, so read them as one picture of the turn.

Then read the bottom row. Card 3 is what you keep and Card 4 is what comes back.

Read Card 5 last. It sits in the middle because it holds the rest of the spread up. A hard top row with a strong center card is a winter you'll get through fine.

## Samhain as the Start of Winter

You'll often see Samhain called the Celtic New Year. That idea started as a Victorian suggestion and spread from there ([the history is here](/halloween-origins-samhain-claims)). The Irish sources treat Samhain as the start of winter, and that's how this spread reads it.

## Tips

Samhain was settling day. "Everyone has debts at Hallow E'en," runs the old saying Kevin Danaher records. If Card 2 or Card 3 points at something you owe, money or otherwise, write it down and settle it.

Come back to the reading at [Imbolc](/st-brigids-day-crosses), the start of spring on February 1st, and see what changed.

Any deck works. A worn deck, a cheap deck, a deck of ordinary playing cards ([how to read those](https://ko-fi.com/s/95f9c29692){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}).
